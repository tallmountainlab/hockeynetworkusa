Youth hockey · Gear

Beginner hockey gear checklist (and how to save on it)

Every piece of gear a new player needs, what to buy new, what's fine used, and how to find skates that fit.

Updated November 1, 2026 · How we make money

Remember wooden sticks? The kind you taped with black tape until the blade was a lumpy mess, then heated over the stove to put a curve in it (and got in trouble for it). Gear has changed a lot since then. It's lighter, safer, and it can get expensive. But your first season doesn't have to be. Hockey has always run on hand-me-downs, garage sales and a cousin's old pads, and that still works.

This list is for kids and adults alike.

Skates that fit

Hockey skates usually run one to one and a half sizes smaller than your shoes. Get fitted in person at a hockey shop. A good fit is snug at the heel, with toes just touching the front when you stand and pulling back a little when you bend your knees. Kids' skates should fit now. "Room to grow" made sense for winter boots, but loose skates make learning much harder.

New skates need sharpening before the first skate. Most Utah rinks and hockey shops will do it for a few dollars, and the hiss of the sharpening wheel is one of hockey's great little sounds.

Choosing a stick

Standing in shoes, the stick should reach somewhere between the chin and the nose. Lower flex numbers are softer and right for kids and lighter adults. Lefty or righty is about feel: most people put their dominant hand at the top, but try both. If you're feeling nostalgic, a wooden stick still works great for beginners, and it's cheap.

Ways to save

  • Beginner gear sets bundle the basics for less.
  • Check used sporting goods stores, team gear swaps and neighborhood buy-sell groups before the season.
  • Ask your program about loaner gear for first-year players.
  • Ask around the family. Somebody's garage has a gear bag in it.
  • Skip top-of-the-line gear for a growing kid. Mid-range lasts as long as they'll fit it.
