Most of us remember our first time on skates. The rental counter, the clunky skates that never quite fit, laces pulled so tight your fingers hurt. Then the first step onto the ice, one hand welded to the boards, while some eight-year-old zipped past like it was nothing. Maybe you fell. Probably you laughed. And maybe, by the end of the night, you let go of the wall.

Whether you're signing up a little one or finally doing it yourself, here's how to get started in Utah.

Start with group lessons

Most Utah rinks run group classes that follow the Learn to Skate USA program, built by U.S. Figure Skating, USA Hockey and US Speedskating. Classes are grouped by level, usually meet once a week for several weeks, and include some practice ice. Once you can stop and turn, many rinks also offer hockey skating classes.

Look for classes at the Salt Lake County rinks, the Utah Olympic Oval, Peaks Ice Arena in Provo, Park City Ice Arena, South Davis Recreation Center, and the rinks in Ogden and North Logan. Our rink directory shows who offers lessons.

Beating the waitlist

Coaches told the Salt Lake Tribune this fall that county learn-to-skate classes have waitlists of several months, and the Acord Ice Center closure has made it tighter. A few ways in:

  • Find out when the next session's registration opens and sign up that day.
  • Join waitlists at two or three rinks, not just the closest one.
  • Look outside Salt Lake County, where classes may have room.
  • Ask about adult-only classes. They often fill more slowly than kids' classes.

One more thing: Salt Lake County no longer allows private coaching during public skate, so private lessons have to be booked on separate ice through the rink.

What to wear

  • Warm, stretchy layers like leggings or track pants and a fleece. Skip the jeans. Once they're wet, they're cold and stiff.
  • Thin socks. Thick socks make skates fit worse, not warmer. (Yes, that goes against everything Grandma told you.)
  • Gloves or mittens, to protect your hands when you fall. And you will fall. Everybody does.
  • A helmet for little kids. Many programs require one, and a bike helmet is often fine to start. A hockey helmet is better.

Rent or buy?

Rent for the first few times. Rental skates are a few dollars at most rinks, and the rental counter is part of the experience. Once you know you're hooked, a pair that fits makes learning much easier. Get fitted at a shop, because hockey skate sizes don't match shoe sizes.

Those first few sessions

Instructors start with how to fall and get back up. Then marching, gliding, and the snowplow stop. Most beginners are moving around the rink on their own within a few weeks. Somewhere in there comes the moment you let go of the wall, and you'll remember it.
